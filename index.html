Cardiac Nexus  /  ECG intelligence

Every heartbeat writes itself down.

A heart is an electrical organ. Roughly once a second it fires a wave of charge through itself, and that wave reaches the skin. Ten seconds, ten electrodes, twelve points of view — and the muscle has told you a great deal about its own condition. This is a machine built to read what it wrote.

01  —  The stakes

The cost of an unread trace.

The heart is the only organ whose failure you can hear coming, if someone is listening. The electrocardiogram is the cheapest, most portable window into cardiac electrical function that medicine has. The scarce resource has never been the machine.

The grammar of a heartbeat

02  —  The signal

Six marks, and the whole of
a heartbeat.

Muscle cells hold a charge across their membranes at rest. When they fire, that separation collapses — and because they fire in sequence rather than all at once, there is always a boundary between charged and discharged tissue. A boundary like that is an electrical dipole. The body is salt water. The chest is the surface. The ECG is what leaks out.

P wave

The atria depolarise.

Small, because there is little muscle up there. Roughly 80 to 100 milliseconds wide. In a healthy sinus beat it points upward in leads II, III and aVF and downward in aVR, because the wavefront travels from upper right to lower left. Its shape is a readout of atrial size.

PR segment

Nothing happens. That is the point.

The flat stretch after the P wave. Almost nothing electrically large enough to see, because the impulse is crawling through the atrioventricular node. This is the only place on the ECG where silence is the measurement — and where the delay lets the atria finish emptying before the ventricles begin to squeeze.

QRS complex

The ventricles fire.

Large, because there is a great deal of muscle. Narrow — under 120 milliseconds — because the Purkinje network delivers the signal almost everywhere at once. Its width is therefore a direct readout of whether the wiring is intact. A wide QRS means the signal crawled through muscle instead of running through fibre.

ST segment

The plateau, and the most loaded 80 ms on the trace.

Every ventricular cell is depolarised at once, so no boundary exists and no voltage difference is generated. It should sit exactly at baseline. When it does not, some region of muscle is at a different potential from its neighbours — which in practice means injured. This is where a heart attack announces itself.

T wave

The ventricles recover.

Normally upright, which is a small mystery until you learn that repolarisation travels the opposite way from depolarisation — outer layer first. Two reversals make a positive. Because recovery is the fragile phase, the T wave is where ischaemia, drug effect, electrolyte disturbance and strain all show up first.

QT interval

The window of vulnerability.

From the start of the QRS to the end of the T wave: the total duration of ventricular electrical activity, and the span during which a badly timed extra beat can tip the heart into a rhythm it does not survive.

03  —  The organ

Five parts, in the order
the current travels.

Everything the ECG records is generated by five structures, firing in sequence. Follow them in order and the trace stops being a squiggle. For each one: what happens here, what goes wrong, what that does to a person, and the mark it leaves on the page.

01

The sinoatrial node

The pacemaker  ·  writes the P wave

What happens here

In the wall of the right atrium sits a strand of tissue a few millimetres across, full of cells that refuse to hold still. Ordinary muscle rests at a stable voltage. These cells leak charge inward continuously, through a current physiologists named funny because it flows the wrong way compared with everything else. Their voltage drifts up until it crosses threshold, fires, resets, and drifts again — about two and a half billion times in a life. Nothing tells it to start. The nervous system only argues about tempo.

What goes wrong

The node ages, fibroses, and loses cells. Ischaemia can starve it, since in most people it is fed by a branch of the right coronary artery. It slows below the body's demand, pauses outright for seconds, or fails to accelerate when a person climbs stairs. Or it is simply outvoted: ectopic tissue elsewhere in the atria begins firing faster.

What it causes

Sinus node dysfunction. Fatigue the patient blames on age, breathlessness on exertion, lightheadedness, then syncope without warning. Its common variant alternates racing and pausing, so the same patient risks both stroke and collapse. It is the leading reason permanent pacemakers are implanted.

The signature

The P wave, and its rhythm. Disease shows as a slow but normal-looking P rhythm, as long flat pauses where a P wave should be, or as P waves that change shape when a different focus takes over. When fibrillation replaces the node entirely, discrete P waves vanish, the baseline becomes a fine tremor, and the intervals lose all regularity.

02

The atria

The receiving rooms  ·  P wave morphology

What happens here

Thin-walled chambers, thin enough in places to see light through. Blood arrives from the veins and mostly falls through them passively. Their muscular contribution comes at the very end of filling, when they squeeze once and push a final bolus across the valves — the atrial kick, worth roughly a fifth of ventricular filling in a healthy adult, and considerably more in a stiff heart.

What goes wrong

Atrial muscle answers sustained pressure by stretching, then by scarring. Hypertension, mitral valve disease, sleep apnoea and obesity all load the atria. Fibrotic tissue conducts slowly and unevenly — precisely the substrate a reentrant circuit needs. The chamber stops contracting as a unit and starts quivering, 300 to 600 times a minute.

What it causes

Atrial fibrillation, and the enlargement that precedes it. Two harms follow. The atrial kick is lost, which can push a stiff ventricle into failure. And blood stagnates in the left atrial appendage, a blind pouch, where clot forms, then leaves, then lodges in the brain. It is one of the most preventable causes of disabling stroke in medicine — and it is often silent until the stroke.

The signature

Chamber size writes itself into the P wave: enlargement broadens it past 120 milliseconds and splits it into two humps. Fibrillation erases it altogether — no organised P waves, a trembling baseline, and an irregularly irregular ventricular response. The trace to the left has lost its P waves.

03

The AV node and
the His–Purkinje system

The wiring  ·  PR interval and QRS width

What happens here

A ring of fibrous tissue insulates the atria from the ventricles completely. There is exactly one electrical door through it, and the atrioventricular node is the doorman. It conducts deliberately slowly — about a tenth of a second — and that delay is not a flaw. It is the pause that lets the atria finish. Past the node the pace changes entirely: insulated fibres carry the signal down the septum, split into left and right bundle branches, and fan into the Purkinje network. The whole ventricular mass is lit in under a tenth of a second, from the inside out.

What goes wrong

Conduction tissue degenerates with age, and fibrosis interrupts it. The block can sit at the node, where escape rhythms are usually reliable, or below it in the His–Purkinje system, where they are not. The opposite failure also exists: an accessory strand that bypasses the node entirely, letting impulses through without the protective delay.

What it causes

First-degree block delays but does not interrupt. Complete heart block is different: the ventricles are left to an escape pacemaker that is slow, unreliable, and capable of stopping. The patient collapses without warning and may not survive it. Bundle branch block rarely causes symptoms directly but marks structural disease — and left bundle branch block makes the two ventricles contract out of step, a dyssynchrony that can drive heart failure on its own.

The signature

This is the interval story. Beyond 200 milliseconds of PR is first-degree block. In complete block the P waves and QRS complexes march at independent rates, related to nothing. Below the node the story is written in width: anything over 120 milliseconds means the ventricles were activated slowly, muscle to muscle, instead of through the network. Watch the complex to the left smear out.

04

The ventricles and
the myocardium

The pump  ·  QRS voltage and the T wave

What happens here

The muscle here is not layered like a barrel but wound in helices, so contraction is a wringing motion and the apex twists against the base. The left ventricle carries a wall about a centimetre thick because it must generate pressure against the entire body; the right, facing only the lungs, is a third as thick. Each beat, calcium floods every cell, the filaments slide, and roughly 60% of the chamber's contents leave.

What goes wrong

Muscle adapts to load, and the adaptations are the disease. Chronic pressure — from hypertension or a narrowed valve — thickens the wall inward, so the chamber becomes strong, stiff, and poorly perfused relative to its own mass. Chronic volume overload dilates it instead. Sarcomere mutations thicken it with no load at all. And after an infarct, dead muscle is replaced by scar, which neither contracts nor conducts evenly.

What it causes

Heart failure, in both its forms: a stiff ventricle that fills poorly and backs pressure into the lungs even while its ejection fraction reads normal, or a dilated one that empties poorly. Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is a leading cause of sudden death in young athletes. And scar-based reentry is the engine of ventricular tachycardia, which degenerates into fibrillation, the terminal rhythm in most sudden cardiac death.

The signature

Mass shows up as voltage — more muscle under the electrode means taller deflections. But the most informative feature is what hypertrophy does to recovery: a downsloping ST segment with an asymmetric inverted T wave, the strain pattern, which says the muscle is not merely large but under duress.

05

The coronary arteries

The supply  ·  the ST segment and Q waves

What happens here

The heart does not feed from the blood inside it. Two arteries leave the aorta millimetres above the valve and wrap the outside of the organ. Vessels branch inward, from the surface toward the lining. Because the muscle compresses its own supply while contracting, the left ventricle is perfused almost entirely in diastole — in the gap between beats. Faster heart rates shorten that gap.

What goes wrong

Lipid accumulates in the vessel wall over decades under a fibrous cap. The narrowing itself is often tolerable. The dangerous event is not gradual: a cap ruptures, the blood meets material it should never contact, and a clot forms in seconds to minutes.

What it causes

Complete occlusion gives ST-elevation myocardial infarction, in which muscle dies at a rate measured in minutes and the outcome depends almost entirely on how fast the vessel is opened. What follows is heart failure from lost contractile mass, arrhythmia from the scar, and in the worst case cardiogenic shock.

The signature

Here the ECG becomes a map as well as a clock. Injury through the full wall thickness pushes the ST segment up, with reciprocal depression in the leads facing from the opposite side. And the leads involved name the artery — V1 to V4 for the anterior wall and the left anterior descending; II, III and aVF for the inferior wall; I, aVL, V5 and V6 for the lateral. Days later, Q waves appear: the permanent handwriting of dead muscle.

04  —  The views

A lead is not a wire.
A lead is a point of view.

Ten electrodes go on the patient: four on the limbs, six across the chest. From those ten contacts, twelve voltage differences are computed — and each one describes the same electrical event projected onto a different axis in space. Asking what a lead sees is the same as asking what a shadow looks like from one angle.

Twelve leads, but only eight independent signals: leads III, aVR, aVL and aVF are exact linear combinations of I and II. The redundancy is not waste. It is how a clinician — and a model — tells a real regional change from a loose electrode.

Hover or focus any lead to read what it looks at. Traces are synthesised from a Gaussian model of the cardiac cycle, projected per lead — they are illustrations of morphology, not recordings of a patient.

05  —  Five failures

What the model is asked
to recognise.

Cardiac Nexus is trained on PTB-XL: 21,388 ten-second recordings from 18,617 patients, annotated by up to two cardiologists. Seventy-one diagnostic statements aggregate into five superclasses — and a single record may carry more than one, because a patient with an old infarct and a bundle branch block has both.

The five carve the ECG along the axis of mechanism, not symptom. Choose one and watch the trace change.

06  —  The difficulty

Why this is hard,
and what we do about it.

Six problems stand between a trace and a trustworthy number. None of them is fully solved here. Each is paired with the specific thing that was done about it.

    07  —  The machine

    Twelve thousand numbers
    become five.

    The input is a 12×1000 array — twelve leads, ten seconds, one hundred samples a second. Time collapses stage by stage while abstraction grows, until the whole recording is a single 128-dimensional vector. Only then does anything ask what the diagnosis is.

    The deliberate seam

    645 parameters out of 6,368,389.

    The classifier head is 0.01% of the model. Everything else is the encoder that turns a waveform into a representation. That split is not an accident of implementation — it is the interface at which a second modality can later be joined, without the ECG branch being rewritten. MRI and tabular records are planned and not started. The seam is the down payment.

    The efficiency argument

    5.2× smaller, 98.1% of the score.

    The published benchmark for this task is 0.928 macro AUROC, achieved by an xresnet1d101 with 33.3 million parameters. This model reaches 0.9107 with 6.4 million, trained on a laptop through Apple's MPS backend. The three-layer CNN that preceded it reached 0.9048 with 59,301 parameters — 0.5% of the published model's size for 97.5% of its score.
